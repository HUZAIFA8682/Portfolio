Executive Summary

Predictive Analysis for the Supply Chain is a proof-of-concept analytics project that demonstrates how predictive analytics can solve two critical e-commerce supply chain problems: product-mismatch returns and last-mile delivery delays.


Built using Altair RapidMiner as the core analytics engine and the comprehensive Olist Brazilian E-Commerce dataset, this project delivers a complete decision-support framework. It helps businesses reduce operational costs, improve customer satisfaction, and optimize inventory placement through clustering, forecasting, and simulation.

The Business Problem

TWO MAJOR CHALLENGES

1. Product-Mismatch Returns

Customers receive items that don't match expectations, leading to costly reverse logistics, restocking fees, and lost revenue. Returns erode profit margins and damage customer trust.

2. Last-Mile Delivery Delays

Late deliveries trigger customer complaints, refund requests, and abandoned carts. In e-commerce, delivery reliability directly correlates with customer loyalty and repeat purchases.

WHY PREDICTIVE ANALYTICS?

Traditional reactive approaches wait for problems to happen. Predictive analytics shifts the mindset from "fix after failure" to "prevent before failure." By analyzing historical transaction, delivery, and review patterns, the system identifies which orders are likely to cause returns or delays—before they happen.

End-to-End Workflow

The complete analytics pipeline from raw data to business insights—built entirely in Altair RapidMiner.

1

Data Collection & Understanding

What happened: Raw Olist datasets were imported into RapidMiner from nine interconnected tables: customers, geolocation, orders, order_items, order_payments, order_reviews, products, sellers, and category_translation.

Why it matters: This multi-table structure represents a real-world e-commerce ecosystem. Integrating these tables creates a 360-degree view of each transaction—from customer location to seller behavior to delivery outcome.

Output: Raw but structured data ready for cleaning.

2

Data Cleaning & Preprocessing

What happened: Data was cleaned, deduplicated, column-selected, renamed, standardized, and date fields were fixed. Separate cleaned datasets were created for each table, then joined into a master analytical dataset.

Why it matters: Raw data cannot be used directly for analysis. This phase transforms fragmented, inconsistent data into a reliable, analysis-ready foundation.

Output: A single, integrated master dataset where each row represents a complete order with customer, product, seller, payment, review, and delivery information.

3

Feature Engineering

What happened: Raw fields were transformed into useful analytical features:

  • Delivery features: actual_delivery_time, estimated_delivery_time, delivery_delay
  • Behavioral features: customer frequency, seller reliability scores, product return indicators
  • Target labels: delivery_risk_target, return_risk_proxy

Important note on return_risk_proxy: The Olist dataset has no direct "return reason" field. Therefore, return risk was modeled as a proxy using review-based dissatisfaction logic—orders with low ratings and negative comment patterns flag mismatch-style returns.

Output: Enriched dataset with predictive features and target labels ready for modeling.

4

Predictive Modeling – Return Risk

What happened: A return-risk modeling workflow was built in RapidMiner. The first working model used Decision Tree as a baseline. The model predicts whether an order/product is likely to be high risk for mismatch-style return behavior.

Why it matters: Before shipping an order, the system can flag high-risk items for quality inspection, packaging verification, or customer confirmation—preventing returns before they happen.

Planned advanced models: XGBoost and Gradient Boosting for stronger prediction accuracy and comparison.

Output: Trained model + scored dataset + performance metrics.

5

Predictive Modeling – Delivery Risk

What happened: A separate modeling track predicts delivery delay risk using delivery-focused features: customer location distance, historical seller performance, product category, and seasonal patterns.

Why it matters: Logistics teams receive alerts for at-risk orders and can proactively expedite shipping, change carriers, or notify customers with updated expectations.

Output: Delivery-risk probability per order + at-risk order identification.

6

Clustering & Location Optimization

What happened: K-Means and X-Means clustering were used to identify customer concentration zones, optimal fulfillment center locations, and seller distribution patterns. Customer segmentation (RFM-style) was also applied.

Why it matters: Returns and delays are often location problems—products stored too far from high-demand customers. Clustering supports decentralized stocking, micro-fulfillment hubs, and reduced transport costs.

Output: Customer location clusters + inventory placement recommendations.

7

Forecasting

What happened: Time-series analysis of historical order volumes with seasonal pattern detection to estimate future demand by region and product category.

Why it matters: Forecasting helps prepare inventory before orders happen—preventing stockouts during peak periods and reducing overstock during slow periods.

Output: Demand forecasts by region/cluster for proactive inventory planning.

8

Simulation / Scenario Analysis

What happened: Simulation compared traditional centralized logistics against improved decentralized stocking strategies. Process Simulator was used to model operational changes without disrupting real operations.

Why it matters: Leadership can see projected ROI of logistics changes before investing in physical infrastructure.

Output: Comparative metrics report + projected improvements.
